Creeping Control, Interlocking Shareholdings, and the Fiduciary Duties of Target Boards Under the SEBI Takeover Code

Devendra Singhania (Visiting Fellow in Commercial Transactions, CCLGFL)

Journal of Corporate and Financial Laws · Vol. 1, Issue 1 (2026) · pp. 159–182 · DOI: 10.1000/jcfl.2026.01.06

Abstract

The Indian market for corporate control operates within a distinctive legal paradigm dominated by promoter-controlled corporate structures and strict statutory takeover regulations. This case comment examines recent contested control battles before the SAT and the Supreme Court, analyzing the legal boundaries of 'creeping acquisitions', the validity of defensive mechanisms adopted by target boards, and the strict neutrality rule under Regulation 26 of the SEBI (Substantial Acquisition of Shares and Takeovers) Regulations, 2011. We critique whether target directors are unduly constrained in shielding company stakeholders from opportunistic hostile bids compared to their common-law peers under Delaware's Unocal doctrine.

I. Introduction: Contested Control in Promoter-Dominated Markets

The Indian market for corporate control has historically been defined by high promoter shareholding blocks, rendering hostile takeover bids comparatively rare. However, the rise of institutional investor activism, pledged share enforcement by lenders, and cross-holding corporate pyramids has created unprecedented opportunities for hostile and creeping acquisitions in listed Indian companies.

II. Regulation 26 and the Board Neutrality Rule

Unlike Delaware jurisprudence, which grants target boards broad latitude under the Unocal and Revlon standards to deploy defensive measures (such as shareholder rights plans or 'poison pills'), Regulation 26 of the SEBI Takeover Regulations imposes an almost absolute neutrality rule. Once a public announcement is made, the target board is statutorily prohibited from issuing unissued shares, entering into material contracts outside the ordinary course, or disposing of material assets without prior approval by special resolution of shareholders.

III. Creeping Acquisitions and Interlocking Shareholdings

Regulation 3(2) permits acquirers holding between 25% and 75% of voting rights to acquire up to 5% additional voting rights per financial year without triggering a mandatory open offer. Where corporate groups utilize cross-holdings, affiliate investment vehicles, and complex derivative collars, identifying when 'effective control' shifts remains a fraught evidentiary question before SEBI and the Securities Appellate Tribunal.

IV. Comparative Perspectives: Delaware vs. India

A comparative analysis with Anglo-American takeover jurisprudence reveals that India's strict shareholder supremacy model prevents incumbent management from entrenching themselves at the expense of shareholder liquidity. However, it also leaves target enterprises vulnerable to opportunistic acquirers seeking short-term arbitrage rather than long-term industrial synergies.

V. Conclusion and Proposed Fiduciary Reforms

The comment concludes by recommending that SEBI introduce a calibrated exception to the neutrality rule permitting independent directors to solicit competing white-knight offers and commission independent enterprise valuation reports to ensure retail shareholders make fully informed tender decisions.
